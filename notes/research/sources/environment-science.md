# Environment: the science

_Research source, 22 October 2026, for `notes/research/environment.md`. What the literature says about shared events, places and weather, with sizes where they exist and what each would mean on a monthly tick. Numbers in brackets point to the list at the end. Where a figure comes from a secondary summary rather than the paper, it says so._

## 1. Shared events and conditions

**People do not panic; they help, mostly each other.** Disaster research since the 1950s (Fritz's Disaster Research Project; Quarantelli at the Disaster Research Center) found panic rare and behaviour in disasters active and prosocial; most first search and rescue is done by survivors, and people converge on the scene rather than flee it [1, 2, 3]. Fritz called the aftermath an "altruistic" community and Barton a "therapeutic community" [2]. Solnit's book gathers the cases (San Francisco 1906, Halifax 1917, Mexico City 1985, 9/11, Katrina) and argues that the violence that does come is more often "elite panic" by officials than disorder by survivors; reviewers fault its generalisation beyond disasters [4].

**The help is unequal and wears off.** Kaniasty and Norris's model: an initial outpouring of mutual help, then a deterioration of support within months; mobilisation is unequal, shaped by social position before the event [5]. After 9/11, blame and scapegoating cut some minority groups out of the community that formed [2].

**Who is left out dies.** Chicago, July 1995, over 700 deaths in a week. In a case-control study of 339 deaths, not leaving home each day carried an odds ratio of 6.7, living alone 2.3, a top floor 4.7, being confined to bed 8.2; social contacts (group activities, friends nearby) protected, as did a working air conditioner (0.3) [6]. Klinenberg's "social autopsy" compared two adjacent neighbourhoods with the same microclimate: about 40 deaths per 100,000 in one and 4 in the other, which he ties to isolation, abandoned streets and withdrawn services, not to heat (from a review; the book names them North and South Lawndale) [7].

**Ties decide recovery.** Aldrich compared Tokyo 1923, Kobe 1995, Tamil Nadu 2004 and New Orleans 2005: the depth of local networks, not the damage or the aid, predicted recovery, by spreading information and help and by keeping people from leaving; he calls social capital "Janus-faced", since strong ties also exclude [8].

**A blackout reveals the state it falls on.** New York, July 1977: an outage after dark during a recession and the Son of Sam summer, with looting of about 2,000 businesses, over 1,000 fires and 3,400–4,500 arrests, concentrated in the poorest neighbourhoods [9, 10]. August 2003: the same city, an outage beginning around 4 p.m. with hours of daylight, little looting and widely reported calm. The explanations offered (daylight, the economy, gentrification, restored civic order, the mood after 9/11) are commentators', not a study's [10, 11]. What both agree on is that the outage did not make the difference; the state of the neighbourhoods did.

**Who caused it decides whether it binds.** After the Exxon Valdez spill, Cordova did not form a therapeutic community: litigation, blame and "recreancy" (the belief that institutions failed their duty) made a "corrosive community", divided and stressed for years [12]. The natural/technological split itself is debated [12]; the robust part is the appraisal: harm with someone to blame splits, harm from no one binds.

**Life turns cluster after.** In the 24 South Carolina counties declared disaster areas after Hurricane Hugo (1989), marriages, births and divorces all rose the following year, against 22 other counties; the rise lasted about a year. Cohan and Cole read it as a life-threatening event pushing people to act on their close relationships [13]. One study, county-level; an association.

**Models of it.** PsychSim's DisasterWorld (Pynadath et al. 2023) simulates population responses to hurricanes with decision-theoretic agents that model each other's beliefs; it was built to generate ground truth for testing social-science inference, not stories [14].

## 2. Places with qualities

**Common space makes neighbours.** In a Chicago public housing development, 145 residents randomly assigned to 18 identical buildings: those whose buildings had trees and grass in the shared space used it more, knew their neighbours better, socialised more and felt safer; use of the space partly explained the ties [15]. The mechanism is a place where people meet, not a mood.

**A bad home is a lasting cause.** Across eight European cities, damp and mould at home went with depression at odds ratios of 1.34–1.44, partly explained by a lower sense of control and by physical health; the author calls it an association [16].

**Restoration is real, short and small to moderate.** A meta-analysis of 32 studies (2,356 participants) found contact with nature raised positive affect moderately and lowered negative affect slightly and consistently, with large heterogeneity [17]. Attention restoration has ambiguous support: a systematic review found measures of attention and restoration inconsistent; a later meta-analysis found small to moderate gains in working memory and cognitive flexibility and less reliable gains in attention [18]. Ulrich's window study: 23 surgical patients with a view of trees against 23 matched patients facing a brick wall, with shorter stays, fewer negative nurse notes and fewer strong painkillers; one hospital, a matched historical comparison [19]. All of these are minutes to days.

**A frightening place follows its reputation.** Visitors to Hampton Court and the Edinburgh vaults reported more unusual experiences in areas reputed to be haunted, unrelated to whether they knew the reputation; experiences also tracked lighting and variance in magnetic fields [20]. A scary house is mostly what is said about it, and its physical cues.

**Attachment.** Lewicka's review of over 400 studies finds place a strong object of attachment despite mobility, with the person studied far more than the place or the process [21]. (Attachment's rise with years of residence, steepest in the first four, comes from studies she reviews, cited second-hand [21].)

## 3. Weather and season as mood

**Daily weather explains very little mood.** Diary study, 1,233 people: temperature, wind and sunlight had main effects on negative affect and sunlight on tiredness, but the average effect was small in variance explained; individual differences were significant (especially for hours of daylight) and not explained by the Big Five, sex or age [22]. Adolescents (497) and their mothers over 30 days fell into four types: about half unaffected; "summer lovers" about 15% of adolescents and 30% of mothers; "summer haters"; and "rain haters", with some concordance between parent and child [23].

**Weather does not move life satisfaction.** Over a million US survey responses matched to local weather: no aspect of the weather had an appreciable effect on life satisfaction; the reliable effects were about 0.02 on a 4-point scale (from a summary of the paper) [24]. Midwestern and Californian students reported the same life satisfaction, while both predicted the Californians would be happier, on climate: a focusing illusion [25]. "Nice weather" and "stunning nature" are what people expect to matter, and do not, at the scale of a life.

**Heat and aggression: real in the field, small.** Across 60 rigorous studies, each standard deviation toward warmer temperatures raised interpersonal violence by about 4% and intergroup conflict by about 14% (median estimates; a later re-analysis finds smaller averages) [26]. US murders and assaults run about 2.6% higher in summer; laboratory results are inconsistent, and routine activity (more people out) competes with heat as the cause [27]. On our town, at the intrigue audit's ~70 hostile acts a world-decade (retaliation 30.6, small acts by people close 38.9), a 4% rise in the hot sixth of months adds under one act a decade.

**Season.** A 2025 meta-analysis of 24 studies (32,866 people) puts winter-pattern seasonal affective disorder at 5.0% and its subsyndromal form at 9.4%, both rising with latitude; the summer pattern at 0.6% [28]. Most prospective population studies find mood dipping in winter; in one Canadian study, major depressive episodes were 70% more frequent in January than August [28]. So season matters for about one person in ten, and for those it is a lasting, renewed condition, not a day's mood.

## What this means on a monthly tick

- A day's weather and a walk by the sea are below the tick: their effects last minutes to days and explain a few per cent of mood. As mood they would be lines with no act behind them, the decoration the feelings audit found (41 kinds, no act).
- What lasts months is a **condition that keeps firing**: a road closed, the power out, a hard winter, a damp home, a dark season for the ~10% prone to it. These match the feelings note's rule that a feeling persists by renewal.
- What changes lives in the shared-event literature is **who helps whom**, **who is left out**, **who is blamed**, and the debts and turns after. All four are decisions between people the engine already has the grammar for.
