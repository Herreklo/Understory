## 1. Life stories: what makes them coherent and compelling

**The life-story model.** Adults hold a narrative identity: a reconstructed past and imagined future that says how they came to be who they are [1, 2]. Interviews ask for **nuclear episodes**: a high point, a low point, a turning point, an earliest memory, and are coded for **agency** (mastery, status, achievement, empowerment) and **communion** (love, friendship, care, belonging) [1].

**Redemption and contamination.** A redemption sequence is a scene that goes from bad to good (loss then growth, failure then recovery); contamination goes from good to bad (a joy spoiled) [3]. Redemption went with well-being and generativity in midlife adults and students; contamination with low well-being in adults; and redemption predicted well-being better than how positive the whole story was [3]. Over four years, agency and redemption predicted improving mental health, contamination worsening; the themes mattered in low points and health scenes, not high points [4]. A review found narrative themes add to traits in predicting well-being, the case strongest for motivational, affective and meaning themes [5]. Across 855 people and 2,565 narratives, three factors cover the field: motivational and affective themes, autobiographical reasoning, and structure; themes relate most reliably to well-being [6]. Typical correlations are small to moderate, r ≈ .1–.3 (inferred from the reviews; no pooled meta-analysis exists).

**Coherence.** Habermas and Bluck name four kinds of global coherence a life story needs: **temporal** (in order), **biographical** (fits the culture's idea of a life's stages), **causal** (how one event led to another and to who I am) and **thematic** (a recurring motif) [7]. Coherence of identity-relevant memories predicted well-being in 103 students, beyond general narrative skill [8].

**Listeners shape stories.** Stories are told in situations; what listeners attend to is what becomes the self [9]. Distracted listeners lead tellers to make less meaning of the same event [10].

**Read with care.** This literature is about the **teller's** well-being. It shows which shapes people use to make sense of their own lives, not which lives others find intriguing. The redemptive self is also a strongly American script [1]: the town is a placeholder, so redemption is a shape to have, not a target. That readers recognise these shapes because they use them on themselves (inferred).

## 2. Tellability: what makes an event worth telling

- **The most reportable event** is the least common one with the greatest effect on the needs and desires of those involved; a story is built backwards from it [11, 12]. Labov's **paradox**: the more reportable an event, the less credible, so tellers lay a chain of ordinary causes back to it [11]. A strange event with no chain reads as false or as noise (inferred).
- **Two bounds.** Norrick: tellability has a floor (enough to warrant attention) and a ceiling, where it tips into the improper and the untellable; tellers signal discomfort as they near it [13].
- **Canonicity and breach.** Bruner: a narrative needs a breach of a canonical script, and the telling makes the breach intelligible [14]. Script research agrees: in stories of routine activities, obstacles, interruptions and deviations are recalled better than the routine actions [15].
- **Engine reading (inferred).** "Expected" has two references: the town's base rate for people in this situation, and this person's own habit. A breach of one's own habit is the one that reveals character; a breach of the town's base rate is the one that is told.

## 3. Interest and curiosity

- **Interest's two appraisals.** Silvia: interest comes from appraised **novelty-complexity** and **coping potential**, the sense that one can understand it. Four in-vivo experiments (polygons, art, poetry), self-report and behaviour; the structure predicted interest and not enjoyment, beyond trait curiosity and openness [16, 17]. Within persons, interest and pleasantness were unrelated: disturbing pictures were highly interesting and unpleasant, calm ones pleasant and dull [18]. **So intrigue is not niceness:** a life can be grim and gripping.
- **Berlyne:** interest is an inverted U of arousal potential (novelty, complexity, uncertainty, conflict); too much reads as aversive confusion [19].
- **The information gap.** Loewenstein: curiosity arises when attention falls on a specific gap in what one knows; it rises with knowledge of the domain, is intense and brief, and often disappoints when satisfied [20]. Curiosity is an inverted U of confidence: highest at middling confidence; curious people paid tokens or time for the answer and remembered surprising answers better one to two weeks later [21]. Curiosity rises with outcome uncertainty while happiness falls [22]. Review: [23].
- **Engine reading (inferred).** An open question works only if the reader knows enough to frame it: a want stated, a pending outcome, a thing one person knows and another does not. A gap the reader cannot see creates nothing.

## 4. Suspense, surprise and curiosity in stories

- **Structural-affect theory.** Brewer and Lichtenstein: what makes a text a story is its discourse order creating **suspense** (the reader knows a threat, not its outcome), **curiosity** (the reader knows an outcome, not how it came about) or **surprise** (a withheld fact revealed). Texts with these structures **and a resolution** were rated more story-like than the same events without them [24]; tested again on Dutch readers [25].
- **Suspense is not 50/50.** A chase scene varied the hero's odds (0, 1, 25, 50, 100 in 100) and liking (neutral to strongly positive): suspense peaked at **1 in 100**, was lowest at certainty either way, and rose with liking [26]. Affective disposition theory: enjoyment rises when liked characters fare well and disliked ones badly [27, 28].
- **Suspense survives knowing.** Readers slowed to verify that Washington became president after a story put an obstacle in his way [29]. Uncertainty is felt, not computed: a dated page can still create suspense on rereading.
- **Spoilers (unstable).** Spoiled short stories were liked slightly more (N = 819) [30]. Then: spoiled stories less moving and transporting (N = 412), especially for readers high in need for affect [31]; spoilers reduced enjoyment (N = 215) [32]; no effect of any spoiler type (N = 326) [33]. Net: knowing the outcome costs little on average; **the how and why carry the interest** (inferred).
- **Mystery.** Enjoyment of a mystery was higher with high uncertainty and curiosity; liking a confirming or surprising ending depended on the reader (self-esteem) (N = 84) [34].
- **Surprise that is inevitable in hindsight.** Surprise is the felt difficulty of explaining an outcome: outcomes that are easy to explain feel less surprising, even when unlikely [35]. Incongruity that resolves is liked more than incongruity that does not [36]; a story interests when it brings the unexpected that can still be integrated, and stops interesting when it cannot be understood [37]. Hindsight: once an outcome is known, people judge it likelier than they did beforehand, without noticing [38]; ease of understanding gets mistaken for prior likelihood, and one causal story crowds out others [39]. **So "inevitable afterwards" = the page already holds a cause the reader can promote to the explanation.** The engine must create the cause before the act; the page must have shown it.
- **Dose of breach.** Folk narratives with mostly intuitive concepts and a minority of counterintuitive ones were remembered better after a week than wholly intuitive or mostly counterintuitive ones, and the culturally successful Grimm tales fit that template [40]. Of 73 world folk tales, 79% had one or two counterintuitive objects, 93% of those only one step from intuitive; the optimum is about one [41]. Later work found the memory advantage partly fragile and separate from belief [42].
- **Shape and volatility.** Period-to-period swings in sentiment predicted better ratings of over 4,000 films (more in thrillers than romance) and more continued reading of 30,000 articles; manipulated volatility raised evaluations [43]. 1,327 Gutenberg stories reduce to six arcs (rise, fall, fall-rise, rise-fall, rise-fall-rise, fall-rise-fall); arcs with reversals were downloaded most [44]. Films and TV episodes that move faster in semantic space are liked more; TV episodes covering more ground are liked less [45]. Across ~40,000 narratives a staging, progression and tension arc appears, but **fitting it did not predict popularity** [46]. Do not force a template.

## 5. Characters a reader follows

- **Transportation** (absorption with imagery, feeling and focus) raised story-consistent beliefs and liking of protagonists, and transported readers found fewer false notes; fact or fiction labels did not matter [47]. A meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes names its antecedents: an **identifiable character**, an **imaginable plot** and **verisimilitude** [48].
- **Identification** (taking the character's goals and view) is distinct from liking or similarity [49].
- **Moral ambiguity costs no engagement.** Morally ambiguous characters were liked less than good ones, but were as transporting, suspenseful and enjoyed; bad characters were liked least yet as transporting (N = 313) [50]. Moral violation related to enjoyment, not to appreciation (N = 294) [51]. **Appreciation** (moved, made to think) is a gratification apart from fun [52]: a sad, unresolved life can be valued without being enjoyed.
- **Goals organise reading.** Readers index events by time, place, protagonist, cause and intention; breaks in any cost processing [53]. Events on the causal chain are recalled more, summarised more and judged more important than dead ends; importance rises with the number of causal links [54, 55]. **A recurring other person** is the protagonist index doing its work: continuity across lines (inferred).
- **Mind-reading load.** Literary fiction plays with nested minds (he knew she thought he wanted) [56]. Adults fail sharply beyond about the fifth order of intentionality [57]. Kidd and Castano's "literary fiction improves theory of mind" [58] failed a three-lab replication (N = 792) [59]; across studies the effect is g = .15 [60]. Use nesting for interest, up to two or three levels; claim no benefit for readers.

## 6. What people retell about others

- About two-thirds of conversation time is social topics [61]; in 467 people's recorded days, gossip was mostly neutral and about social information, not malicious [62]. Gossip is cultural learning: stories of how someone came to grief by breaking a norm, or did well [63].
- **Social information travels better.** Along chains of retelling, information about third-party relationships was passed on more accurately and fully than equivalent non-social information; everyday social interactions did as well as intense gossip [64]. Content biases (emotional, social, threat, counterintuitive) act at choosing, remembering and passing on [65].
- **Emotion picks what spreads.** People pass on social anecdotes that arouse interest, surprise, disgust or happiness, depending on audience [66]; urban legends that disgust more are passed on more [67]. Per 1 SD, anger raised the odds of a New York Times article being most-emailed by 34%, awe 30%, anxiety 21%, practical value 30%, interest 25%, surprise 14%; sadness lowered them 16% [68]. Arousal, not valence.
- **Weak:** that negative gossip makes faces dominate vision [69] did not replicate [70]. People judge others as strongly from gossip marked untrustworthy as from fact [71] (relevant to reputation, not yet built).
- Rimé's sharing figures are in `feelings-science.md` §4.

## 7. How lives are judged from their shape

- **James Dean effect.** A wonderful life ending abruptly was rated better than the same life with extra mildly pleasant years; a terrible life with extra moderately bad years was rated better than one ending abruptly; adding years in the middle mattered less than adding them at the end. Duration was neglected [72].
- Posthumous reversals shift ratings of a life's goodness and happiness, unrelated to how engaging the life was as a story [73]. The order of the same events changes how meaningful a life is judged; morality weighs heavily [74]. People prefer improving sequences [75], and satisfaction tracks the rate of change, not only the level [76].
- **Peak-end, pooled:** r ≈ .58 with retrospective evaluations, duration ≈ 0 (174 samples), but the plain average does about as well [77]. A vacation study found peak-end no strong predictor and memory heavily reconstructed [78]; in four experience-sampling studies (N = 1,889) peaks and ends were overweighted, depending on how the question was framed [79].
- **Read with care.** These are judgments of how **good** a life was. Interest is a different appraisal [18]. The end of a page will colour what a reader concludes about the person (inferred); it does not make the page more intriguing.

## 8. Against forced drama

Every line of evidence has a ceiling: Norrick's improper [13], Berlyne's aversive arousal [19], coping potential [16], Kintsch's incomprehensible [37], one breach per tale [41], TV that covers too much ground [45], no reward for a template [46], Labov's incredible [11]. And `WHY.md`: ordinary routines are part of a convincing world. A breach needs a canonical background to breach. Our own readings already show readers choose the version where more happens (70% when five or more lines apart) and score kinds of thing over length (`docs/method/reading.md`): a round that adds drama must show the effect survives with lines and kinds as terms.

## 10. Weak spots

- **No direct study of what makes another person's whole ordinary life intriguing.** Everything here transfers from self-narrative (teller well-being), fiction enjoyment, or cultural transmission. The transfer is inferred.
- Effects on enjoyment are mostly single experiments with students, often in the US. Arc and volatility studies are correlational on popularity (except [43]'s experiments). Spoilers, theory of mind, gossip vision and the counterintuitive optimum have failed or mixed replications.
- Narrative-identity correlations are small (r ≈ .1–.3) and about the teller.
- Peak-end evidence is about evaluation, not interest.

## 11. This fails if …

1. **… the gain is only more happening.** Effect with lines and kinds of thing as terms ≈ 0 (the scorer's third and fourth terms).
2. **… breaches come without causes.** Share of breaching events whose page line names a cause shown on an earlier line < 70%, or readers' faults of the "can't follow / unmotivated" kind per ten lines rise.
3. **… reversals become the rule.** Share of lives with a caused reversal in ten years above about a half, or kinds per life fall: drama turns samey.
4. **… surprise is not recognised afterwards.** In a predict-then-explain test, readers either predict the act from the lines before it (no surprise) or cannot name the earlier line that explains it afterwards (no hindsight).
5. **… a want without stake reads like no want.** Round 25d's yearly want (+0.14) repeated: pursuits up, effect inside the null.
